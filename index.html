One machine, seven stations, one gate.

The plan, drawn: what wakes up on a schedule, what shares state, where work stops for a human yes, and the order to build it in so the machine earns each next part. Nothing here sells on its own.

01

General arrangement

Plan view · eight stations · one shared state file · one decision point

Seven scheduled jobs, one shared state file, one valve. Each station wakes, reads the pipeline, makes a single artifact, writes it back and stops. Nothing runs continuously, and nothing touches the state freehand.

Drag the drawing sideways. It does not shrink below legibility.

The only exit from this machine toward a stranger's inbox is the valve at D: your hand, your yes. Volume is the machine's job; the last inch is yours, and the three notes below are the whole argument for why that stays true.

  1. The bus is the office

    A Scout run that never reaches the pipeline did not happen. Six agents editing one file at once is how these stacks rot, so every station writes through a script and reads only what the schema allows. The state file is the memory; the drawing is just the plan of it.

  2. The valve is the business

    Cold mail, quotes, publishes, invoices: all of it waits at D. Approve, edit, or kill, in seconds. That is why the machine can run at volume without reading like spam: nothing leaves without a human signature on it.

  3. The alarm is the honesty

    A station that returns nothing for three days gets reported. Silent agent death and "no new leads today" look identical otherwise, and you find out weeks later that the machine stopped running in March.

03

Duty cycle

24 hours · five wake-ups · one weekly reckoning

The day is a fixed rotation, not a queue your prompts feed. Each run is cost-bounded: a ceiling on pages audited or drafts written, so a slow week cannot quietly turn into a large bill. Anything the rotation cannot finish waits for tomorrow. That is the point of a schedule.

04

Erection sequence

One station at a time · each week's gate is a number, not a feeling

Seven agents built at once is how this ends up as a beautiful machine automating the wrong step. So the sequence is a chain of proofs: each week's station has to beat what your hands already do before the next one is allowed to exist.

  1. Week 01

    State file + Scout, read-only

    The pipeline comes first, because everything downstream is a write to it. Scout only reads the world and appends.

    Proves: 50 qualified leads in your niche and cantons, each carrying a pain signal you would have noticed yourself.

  2. Week 02

    Auditor

    The ammunition layer. If the briefs are not something you would send under your own name, the machine has not earned the next station.

    Proves: briefs you would send by hand, in German, with three findings specific enough to argue with.

  3. Week 03

    Scribe + the approval queue

    The first station that reaches toward a stranger, and it reaches through you. Nothing leaves without a yes.

    Proves: reply rate at or above your manual baseline. Below it, the drafting is the problem, not the volume.

  4. Week 04

    Closer-assist + Builder

    Only worth building once outreach produces conversations. Earlier, it automates a step nothing is reaching.

    Proves: a quote and a preview you could put in front of a client in an hour, not a day.

  5. After

    Ops + retainers

    Fed by the first paying client, not by optimism. This is where recurring revenue lives, so it is built last and kept running.

    Proves: a monthly report you would be happy to have written, and a site you noticed was down before the client did.

Stop rule: if a stage does not beat your manual baseline, stop and fix that stage. Adding stations to a broken step just makes the breakage faster and more expensive.

05

Drawing notes

Revision A · assumptions stated rather than buried

What this machine is not

  • Not an autonomous seller. Agent-only cold outreach at volume reads like spam within a week. The gate exists because the last inch is judgment, not throughput.
  • Not a builder that ships alone. Builder makes previews; publishing stays a human act, and QA can block a handover but not approve one.
  • Not a shortcut past list quality. The build is the fast part. Deliverability and list quality decide whether any of it earns, and no amount of agents fixes a bad list.

What the machine needs first

  • Niche and cities. Which trades, which cantons. Scout cannot score what it cannot name.
  • Your current lead source. So the baseline is a real number, not an impression.
  • Price points. Three tiers in CHF, so a quote is a fill-in, not an essay.
  • Channel and access. Cold mail, letters, LinkedIn, walk-ins, plus the mailbox and calendar that Ops would read.
